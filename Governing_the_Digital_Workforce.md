# Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is entering a new stage of enterprise adoption.

For many organizations, AI began as a productivity tool capable of generating content, answering questions, summarizing information, and supporting decisions. Governance frameworks developed during this period focused primarily on data protection, model transparency, responsible use, ethics, cybersecurity, and regulatory compliance.

The emergence of agentic AI changes the operating model.

AI systems are increasingly capable of planning tasks, coordinating workflows, interacting with enterprise applications, invoking tools, and executing operational activities with limited human intervention. Rather than simply producing information, these systems are beginning to perform work on behalf of organizations.

As organizations deploy more AI agents, they are creating a new operational capability: a **digital workforce**.

This changes the governance question.

The issue is no longer limited to whether an AI system produces an accurate output. Organizations must also determine how authority is delegated, how work is supervised, how agent activity is monitored, when people must intervene, and who remains accountable for outcomes.

Traditional technology governance remains necessary, but it is no longer sufficient on its own.

Agentic AI requires a management-oriented approach.

This framework proposes that AI agents should be governed as operational actors working within clearly defined authority boundaries. The objective is not to humanize AI or assign it legal status. It is to apply established management disciplines: delegation, supervision, accountability, control, and continuous improvement, to systems that increasingly perform work.

The central principle of this framework is straightforward:

> **AI agents may perform work, but only people remain accountable for the work performed.**

---

# 1. Purpose

The purpose of this framework is to provide a practical management model for the responsible deployment and supervision of AI agents.

It is intended to help organizations:

- recognize AI agents as an emerging digital workforce;
- define how authority may be delegated to AI systems;
- align agent autonomy with human accountability;
- establish proportionate supervision and intervention mechanisms;
- classify agents according to operational authority and impact;
- strengthen governance as both agent capability and organizational maturity evolve; and
- scale agentic AI without allowing operational responsibility to become unclear.

This framework is designed as a strategic reference. Organizations may adapt it to their own governance structures, operating models, legal obligations, technology environments, and risk tolerances.

It does not replace applicable laws, regulations, contractual obligations, cybersecurity controls, privacy requirements, employment policies, professional standards, or sector-specific governance.

---

# 2. Why This Framework Matters

Every major technological shift changes how organizations work.

The personal computer changed how individuals created and processed information.

The internet changed how organizations communicated and coordinated.

Cloud computing changed where enterprise systems operated.

Artificial intelligence is changing how work itself is performed.

Historically, enterprise software has been treated as a tool. Applications execute predefined instructions, databases store records, and automation platforms perform structured workflows. Organizations govern these systems through architecture standards, access controls, cybersecurity practices, change management, and operational procedures.

Agentic AI does not fit neatly within that model.

An AI agent may be capable of:

- interpreting an objective;
- determining intermediate steps;
- retrieving and synthesizing information;
- invoking software tools;
- interacting with multiple systems;
- initiating or completing workflows;
- adapting its approach based on results; and
- acting with varying degrees of operational independence.

These capabilities create a different relationship between the organization and the technology.

The governance question is no longer only:

> **How should organizations govern AI systems?**

It increasingly becomes:

> **How should organizations govern work performed by a combination of human employees and autonomous digital workers?**

This distinction changes how organizations should think about authority, supervision, accountability, performance, escalation, and control.

Technology governance can define system standards.

Management governance must define how work is assigned, supervised, and owned.

# 3. The Digital Workforce Concept

Organizations coordinate human work through roles, responsibilities, authority structures, processes, supervision, and accountability.

Agentic AI introduces a second operational workforce.

## 3.1 Human Workforce

The human workforce consists of employees, contractors, leaders, specialists, and other people who perform cognitive or operational tasks within defined organizational roles.

Human workers may exercise judgment, assume professional duties, make authorized decisions, and remain accountable for their conduct.

## 3.2 Digital Workforce

The digital workforce consists of AI agents capable of executing tasks, interacting with systems, supporting workflows, or performing operational actions on behalf of an organization.

Digital workers may produce outputs and execute actions, but they do not:

- possess independent organizational authority;
- assume legal or professional responsibility;
- determine their own legitimate business purpose;
- define the limits of their own access;
- approve their own deployment; or
- become accountable for the consequences of their actions.

The term **digital workforce** is therefore an operational management concept, not a legal or anthropomorphic classification.

## 3.3 From Software Tools to Operational Actors

Traditional software generally performs a predefined function when instructed.

AI agents may pursue an objective through multiple steps and make operational choices within the constraints of their design.

For governance purposes, this makes them operational actors.

An operational actor is a system that can perform actions affecting data, workflows, systems, communications, or business outcomes. Its authority must therefore be explicitly defined rather than inferred from the permissions of the person using it.

AI agents should operate under:

- a clearly defined purpose;
- an approved operational scope;
- dedicated identity and access permissions;
- transparent execution and logging;
- named human ownership;
- appropriate supervisory oversight;
- intervention and escalation mechanisms; and
- periodic review of performance and authority.

The introduction of digital workers does not reduce the need for management.

It extends management into a new operational domain.

# 4. The Management Model

This framework proposes five principles for managing the digital workforce:

1. Delegated Authority
2. Progressive Supervision
3. Accountability Without Transfer
4. Controlled Operational Independence
5. Continuous Governance Evolution

These principles are intended to operate together. Authority without supervision creates uncontrolled autonomy. Accountability without intervention rights creates responsibility without control. Static governance becomes ineffective as agent capabilities and operating conditions change.

---

# 5. Principle 1: Delegated Authority

AI agents act on behalf of an organization.

Their authority must therefore be delegated explicitly.

An agent should not be permitted to act merely because a human user has access to a system or because a technical integration makes an action possible. Technical capability does not constitute organizational authorization.

Each agent should have a defined operational mandate that establishes:

- the purpose for which the agent exists;
- the tasks it is permitted to perform;
- the systems and tools it may access;
- the data it may retrieve, create, modify, or transmit;
- the actions it may execute without prior approval;
- the actions requiring human authorization;
- the conditions under which execution must stop; and
- the person or function accountable for its operation.

Agents should not inherit unrestricted human permissions.

They should operate through dedicated machine identities and permission structures designed for their specific role.

Delegated authority should follow the principle of least privilege. An agent should receive only the access and action rights necessary to perform its approved work.

As the agent's role changes, its authority should be reviewed and adjusted. New capabilities, integrations, tools, or deployment contexts may materially increase operational authority even when the agent's original purpose appears unchanged.

**Key message**

> **An AI agent should never possess more authority than the organization has deliberately assigned to it.**

---

# 6. Principle 2: Progressive Supervision

AI agents should be supervised according to their maturity, reliability, operational authority, and potential impact.

New agents should generally begin with closer supervision.

During early deployment, supervisors may:

- review intermediate steps;
- validate outputs before use;
- approve sensitive actions;
- monitor execution in real time;
- test failure and fallback behavior;
- compare results against expected outcomes; and
- investigate unexpected patterns.

As reliability improves and the organization develops experience managing the workflow, supervision may shift toward periodic review, exception handling, and outcome-based monitoring.

This progression should not be automatic.

Reduced supervision should be supported by evidence, including:

- stable operational performance;
- acceptable error and exception rates;
- effective monitoring;
- tested intervention mechanisms;
- clear escalation procedures;
- appropriate access controls; and
- demonstrated supervisory capability.

Oversight should never disappear entirely.

Even mature agents require periodic audit and reassessment because models, tools, data sources, integrations, and operating environments change over time.

Progressive supervision allows organizations to balance efficiency with control. It avoids two common failures: permanently requiring human review of every low-impact action, and granting broad autonomy before reliability and governance are established.

**Key message**

> **Supervision may become lighter as confidence grows, but it should never become absent.**

---

# 7. Principle 3: Accountability Without Transfer

Work may be delegated to an AI agent.

Responsibility cannot be transferred to it.

AI agents do not assume accountability for the outputs they generate or the actions they execute. Accountability remains with the people and organizational functions that approve, deploy, operate, supervise, or rely upon the agent.

This responsibility is not limited to formal managers.

Whenever a person uses an AI agent to perform work, that person is delegating work and assumes a supervisory responsibility appropriate to the context.

Organizations should therefore define:

- an accountable business owner for each agent;
- an operational supervisor for day-to-day performance;
- a technical owner for system integrity and maintenance;
- escalation responsibilities for incidents and abnormal behavior; and
- decision authority for changing, suspending, or retiring the agent.

Accountability must be matched with intervention authority.

A person cannot reasonably be held responsible for an agent if that person lacks the ability to:

- observe what the agent is doing;
- pause or stop execution;
- reject or override an action;
- restrict access;
- escalate a concern;
- initiate incident response; or
- remove the agent from operation.

AI-enabled workflows should therefore remain interruptible by design.

Quality and control should be built into the workflow rather than inspected only after execution has completed.

**Key message**

> **Using an AI agent is an act of delegation, and delegation does not remove responsibility for the outcome.**

---

# 8. Principle 4: Controlled Operational Independence

Agentic AI is valuable because it can operate with a degree of independence.

That independence must remain controlled.

Autonomy should not be understood as unrestricted freedom. It is the bounded ability of an agent to act within approved operational, technical, and governance constraints.

Controlled operational independence requires that:

- agent activity is visible and traceable;
- execution remains within approved workflow boundaries;
- system access is limited to the agent's role;
- high-impact actions are subject to appropriate approval;
- abnormal behavior can be detected;
- execution can be paused or stopped;
- fallback procedures are available; and
- outcomes can be reviewed after execution.

The appropriate degree of independence depends on the nature of the work.

An agent drafting an internal summary may operate with broader flexibility because a human reviews the output before use.

An agent modifying enterprise records, communicating externally, or initiating a financial action requires much stronger constraints.

Operational independence should therefore be proportionate to:

- the agent's capability;
- the reversibility of its actions;
- the sensitivity of the data involved;
- the scope of system access;
- the potential effect on people;
- the potential financial, legal, operational, or reputational impact; and
- the organization's ability to detect and correct failure.

**Key message**

> **Autonomy is useful only when it remains visible, bounded, and interruptible.**

---

# 9. Principle 5: Continuous Governance Evolution

Digital workforce governance should be treated as a continuous management system, not a one-time approval exercise.

AI agents change over time.

Models may be updated.

Prompts, tools, workflows, integrations, and data sources may change.

Business processes may expand.

An agent initially deployed for a narrow purpose may gradually acquire broader operational influence.

The organization also changes.

Supervisors gain experience.

Monitoring improves.

New failure patterns emerge.

Governance structures mature.

For these reasons, organizations should periodically reassess:

- whether the agent's purpose remains valid;
- whether its capability has expanded;
- whether its permissions remain appropriate;
- whether its risk and impact classification has changed;
- whether monitoring remains sufficient;
- whether supervisors have the required expertise and authority;
- whether intervention procedures remain effective; and
- whether the agent should be expanded, constrained, redesigned, suspended, or retired.

Governance should evolve alongside both technology capability and organizational learning.

**Key message**

> **Digital workforce governance is a management cycle, not an approval event.**

# 10. The Dynamic Governance Model

Traditional governance frameworks often classify systems primarily by technical capability or risk.

Agentic AI introduces another critical variable: the organization's ability to manage the system.

Effective governance depends on the interaction of two dimensions:

## 10.1 Agent Capability

Agent capability describes the maturity and operational scope of the AI agent.

It includes the agent's ability to:

- interpret objectives;
- plan and sequence tasks;
- use tools;
- interact with enterprise systems;
- create or modify data;
- initiate actions;
- communicate externally; and
- operate with limited human involvement.

As capability increases, the agent may create greater operational value. It may also create greater impact when errors, misuse, or unexpected behavior occur.

## 10.2 Managerial Maturity

Managerial maturity describes the organization's ability to supervise and govern AI agents effectively.

It includes the ability to:

- define roles and operational boundaries;
- assign accountable ownership;
- understand how agents plan and execute work;
- monitor behavior and outcomes;
- detect anomalies;
- manage permissions;
- intervene during execution;
- investigate failures;
- maintain auditability; and
- adjust governance based on experience.

An advanced agent deployed into an organization with limited managerial maturity may create significant unmanaged risk.

A mature organization may be able to operate more capable agents responsibly because it has established effective supervisory practices, technical controls, and escalation mechanisms.

## 10.3 Agent Capability × Managerial Maturity

| | **Lower Managerial Maturity** | **Higher Managerial Maturity** |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower Agent Capability** | **Controlled experimentation:** narrow use cases, close review, limited permissions, and deliberate learning. | **Managed scaling:** standardized controls, broader adoption of low-impact agents, and outcome-based monitoring. |
| **Higher Agent Capability** | **Governance mismatch:** constrain deployment, reduce authority, or strengthen management capability before scaling. | **Progressive delegation:** carefully expand autonomy where monitoring, ownership, intervention, and evidence support it. |

The objective is not to move every agent toward maximum autonomy.

The objective is to maintain alignment between the authority of the agent and the organization's ability to manage it.

# 11. Progressive Delegation

Because agent capability and managerial maturity evolve, organizations should adopt a model of progressive delegation.

Under this model, operational authority expands gradually as evidence and governance capability improve.

Progressive delegation may follow this sequence:

1. **Observe:** the agent proposes work, and a human performs the action.
2. **Approve:** the agent prepares the action, and a human authorizes execution.
3. **Supervise:** the agent executes within a defined workflow while a human monitors.
4. **Review by exception:** the agent executes routine actions, while exceptions and anomalies are escalated.
5. **Evaluate by outcome:** mature, lower-volatility activity is assessed through performance measures, periodic audit, and targeted review.

Movement between stages should depend on demonstrated performance and control effectiveness, not on enthusiasm for automation.

Organizations should also be prepared to move an agent backward when:

- performance deteriorates;
- operational scope changes;
- permissions expand;
- incidents occur;
- models or integrations are materially updated;
- monitoring becomes insufficient; or
- the operating environment becomes more sensitive.

Progressive delegation is therefore reversible.

# 12. Digital Workforce Classification

Organizations need a practical structure for classifying AI agents and determining proportionate governance.

This framework proposes four digital workforce levels.

The levels are based primarily on:

- operational authority;
- system access;
- ability to modify data or execute actions;
- external interaction; and
- potential impact.

These levels are not legal or regulatory classifications. Organizations should apply any separate risk, privacy, cybersecurity, safety, or regulatory assessment required by their context.

## 12.1 Level 1: Digital Assistant

### Characteristics

Digital Assistants primarily support information work. They produce content, insights, recommendations, or summaries but do not directly modify enterprise systems or execute operational decisions.

### Typical activities

- document summarization;
- internal knowledge retrieval;
- drafting and rewriting;
- research support;
- analytical assistance; and
- preparation of recommendations.

### Governance focus

- responsible use;
- data handling;
- output review;
- appropriate disclosure; and
- user accountability.

### Typical safeguards

- approved-tool requirements;
- data sensitivity restrictions;
- human review before operational use; and
- basic usage logging where appropriate.

---

## 12.2 Level 2: Workflow Worker

### Characteristics

Workflow Workers execute structured, predefined processes and may interact with approved internal systems or tools.

They may complete multiple steps autonomously, but their operating domain remains narrow and controlled.

### Typical activities

- internal reporting automation;
- data preparation;
- document routing and processing;
- structured case handling;
- task orchestration across internal systems; and
- preparation of system updates for approval.

### Governance focus

- workflow boundaries;
- permissions;
- execution traceability;
- exception handling; and
- periodic operational review.

### Typical safeguards

- dedicated agent identity;
- least-privilege access;
- approved workflow definitions;
- logging of agent actions;
- monitoring of exceptions; and
- human approval for material or irreversible actions.

---

## 12.3 Level 3: Operational Worker

### Characteristics

Operational Workers interact directly with enterprise systems and may modify records, execute internal processes, or take actions that affect business operations.

### Typical activities

- updating customer relationship management records;
- coordinating inventory or logistics activity;
- executing approved internal transactions;
- managing structured operational workflows;
- modifying enterprise data; and
- triggering downstream processes.

### Governance focus

- formal deployment approval;
- operational ownership;
- action-level traceability;
- monitoring and anomaly detection;
- intervention rights; and
- incident response.

### Typical safeguards

- clearly defined operational mandate;
- strict identity and access controls;
- action thresholds and approval checkpoints;
- tested stop and rollback mechanisms;
- documented escalation procedures; and
- periodic control review.

---

## 12.4 Level 4: Autonomous Representative

### Characteristics

Autonomous Representatives interact with customers, partners, external services, financial systems, public platforms, or other environments where agent actions may create significant external consequences.

They may operate browser-based or computer-use workflows and may execute high-impact actions with limited human involvement.

### Typical activities

- communicating directly with customers or partners;
- negotiating or committing within approved limits;
- executing financial or commercial transactions;
- interacting with external platforms;
- operating across multiple connected systems; and
- representing the organization in an external workflow.

### Governance focus

- executive risk acceptance;
- strict authority limits;
- real-time or near-real-time monitoring;
- external communication controls;
- strong intervention mechanisms;
- resilience and fallback;
- legal and regulatory review; and
- continuous performance evaluation.

### Typical safeguards

- executive-level approval;
- dedicated machine identity;
- narrowly scoped permissions;
- transaction or commitment limits;
- pre-approved communication boundaries;
- real-time anomaly detection;
- immediate suspension capability;
- comprehensive audit trails; and
- formal incident and recovery procedures.

# 13. Governance Escalation by Workforce Level

As agents move from lower to higher workforce levels, governance should become more structured.

| Governance Area | **Level 1** | **Level 2** | **Level 3** | **Level 4** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Human oversight | Review before use | Periodic review and approval checkpoints | Outcome monitoring and exception handling | Continuous or near-real-time supervision |
| System access | None or minimal | Limited internal access | Direct enterprise-system access | Internal and external access |
| Action authority | Advisory | Constrained workflow actions | Operational actions | High-impact or externally visible actions |
| Approval | Standard tool/use approval | Workflow and access approval | Formal deployment approval | Executive and cross-functional approval |
| Monitoring | Basic usage visibility | Execution logging | Detailed action monitoring | Real-time observability and anomaly detection |
| Intervention | User rejects output | Pause workflow | Stop, override, rollback, escalate | Immediate suspension, containment, and recovery |
| Review frequency | Periodic | Risk-based | Scheduled and event-triggered | Continuous plus formal periodic review |

A higher workforce level does not mean that an agent is more desirable.

It means that the agent possesses greater operational authority and therefore requires stronger management.

# 14. Governance Structure

Digital workforce governance should operate across three complementary layers:

1. Executive Governance
2. Governance Review
3. Operational Oversight

## 14.1 Executive Governance

Executive leadership establishes the strategic direction and governance boundaries for the digital workforce.

Responsibilities may include:

- defining organizational objectives for agentic AI;
- establishing risk appetite;
- approving principles and policies;
- determining prohibited or restricted uses;
- approving high-impact deployments;
- reviewing systemic and portfolio-level risks; and
- ensuring adequate resources for governance and supervision.

These responsibilities may be coordinated through an AI Council, executive committee, or equivalent governance body.

Executive governance should focus on strategic boundaries and material decisions rather than day-to-day management of individual agents.

## 14.2 Governance Review

The governance review function translates enterprise principles into deployment decisions.

Responsibilities may include:

- classifying proposed agents;
- reviewing purpose and operational scope;
- evaluating system and data access;
- confirming accountable ownership;
- reviewing monitoring and intervention mechanisms;
- assessing legal, privacy, cybersecurity, safety, and compliance considerations;
- verifying readiness before deployment; and
- defining conditions for approval.

Participants may include representatives from:

- business operations;
- information technology;
- enterprise architecture;
- cybersecurity;
- data governance;
- privacy;
- legal and compliance;
- risk management; and
- relevant subject-matter functions.

The review should be proportionate to the agent's authority and impact.

## 14.3 Operational Oversight

Operational oversight is the day-to-day management of deployed agents.

Responsibilities may include:

- monitoring agent activity;
- reviewing outcomes and anomalies;
- maintaining operational documentation;
- validating ongoing performance;
- managing permissions;
- escalating incidents;
- initiating intervention;
- coordinating updates; and
- confirming that the agent remains within scope.

Operational oversight should normally sit with the function responsible for the workflow supported by the agent.

The operational team acts as the immediate supervisor of the digital worker.

## 14.4 Cross-Functional Collaboration

No single department can govern the digital workforce alone.

Operational teams understand the work.

Technical teams understand the systems.

Security teams protect infrastructure and access.

Legal, privacy, compliance, and risk teams interpret obligations.

Leadership defines strategic direction and risk tolerance.

Effective governance requires clear communication, defined decision rights, and reliable escalation pathways across these functions.

# 15. Operational Safeguards

Governance principles become effective only when they are implemented through operational safeguards.

## 15.1 Agent Identity

Every deployed agent should operate under a clearly identifiable machine identity.

Shared human credentials should not be used for autonomous execution.

A dedicated identity enables the organization to:

- attribute actions to a specific agent;
- apply role-specific permissions;
- separate human and machine activity;
- maintain audit trails;
- investigate incidents; and
- suspend the agent without disabling a human user's access.

The identity should be linked to the agent's purpose, owner, operational scope, and lifecycle status.

## 15.2 Access Control and Operational Boundaries

Agent permissions should follow least privilege.

Boundaries may include:

- systems the agent may access;
- data categories it may retrieve;
- records it may create or modify;
- tools it may invoke;
- actions it may execute;
- transaction or value limits;
- approved communication channels;
- external domains or parties it may contact;
- time periods during which it may operate; and
- conditions requiring approval.

Permissions should be reviewed after material changes and periodically throughout the agent's lifecycle.

## 15.3 Monitoring and Observability

Organizations should maintain sufficient visibility to understand what an agent is doing and why operational outcomes occurred.

Monitoring may include:

- prompts or objectives received;
- plans or steps generated;
- tools invoked;
- systems accessed;
- data retrieved or modified;
- actions executed;
- approvals obtained;
- exceptions encountered;
- outputs produced; and
- escalation or intervention events.

The level of detail should be proportionate to the agent's authority, risk, and operating context.

Monitoring should support both operational management and post-incident investigation.

## 15.4 Human Intervention

Human supervisors should retain practical authority to intervene.

Intervention mechanisms may include:

- approval checkpoints;
- pause controls;
- stop controls;
- access revocation;
- action rejection;
- workflow override;
- rollback or reversal;
- fallback to a manual process; and
- escalation to technical, security, legal, or executive functions.

Intervention mechanisms should be tested, not merely documented.

## 15.5 Change and Lifecycle Control

Material changes should trigger reassessment.

Examples include:

- model replacement or major version change;
- new tools or integrations;
- expanded system access;
- new data sources;
- increased action authority;
- external communication capability;
- movement into a new business process;
- changes affecting people or regulated activity; and
- significant incidents or performance deterioration.

Agents should also have a defined retirement process covering access revocation, data handling, documentation retention, and transition of the supported workflow.

# 16. Minimum Agent Deployment Record

Before deployment, each agent should have a documented management record.

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Agent name and identifier | Unique name and machine identity |
| Business purpose | The organizational objective the agent supports |
| Accountable owner | Person or function answerable for deployment and outcomes |
| Operational supervisor | Person or team responsible for day-to-day oversight |
| Workforce level | Digital Assistant, Workflow Worker, Operational Worker, or Autonomous Representative |
| Approved tasks | Work the agent is permitted to perform |
| Prohibited actions | Actions the agent must not perform |
| System access | Systems, tools, and environments the agent may access |
| Data access | Data categories the agent may use |
| Action authority | Actions permitted independently and actions requiring approval |
| External interaction | Approved customers, partners, platforms, or channels |
| Monitoring | Logs, metrics, alerts, and review processes |
| Intervention | Pause, stop, override, rollback, and escalation mechanisms |
| Performance measures | Expected quality, reliability, timeliness, and exception thresholds |
| Review date | Date for periodic governance reassessment |
| Retirement conditions | Events or criteria requiring suspension or decommissioning |

This record is not intended to become a heavy administrative artifact. Its purpose is to make authority, ownership, and control explicit before an agent begins performing work.

# 17. Building a Digital Workforce

Organizations should build digital workforce capability gradually.

## Step 1: Identify the Work

Begin with a clearly defined workflow or task.

The organization should understand:

- the objective;
- the current human process;
- expected outputs;
- decision points;
- exceptions;
- dependencies; and
- consequences of failure.

Automation should not begin with an undefined process.

## Step 2: Assign Ownership

Every agent should have an accountable business owner and an operational supervisor.

Ownership should be established before technical deployment.

## Step 3: Classify the Agent

Determine the workforce level based on authority, access, external interaction, and potential impact.

Apply any additional risk, privacy, cybersecurity, safety, or regulatory assessment required by the organization.

## Step 4: Define Boundaries

Document permitted tasks, prohibited actions, system access, data access, approval thresholds, and escalation conditions.

## Step 5: Establish Safeguards

Implement identity, least-privilege access, logging, monitoring, approval checkpoints, stop mechanisms, fallback procedures, and incident response.

## Step 6: Pilot Under Close Supervision

Begin with constrained authority and frequent review.

Test expected operation, exceptions, failure modes, and intervention mechanisms.

## Step 7: Evaluate Evidence

Assess reliability, quality, operational impact, anomaly patterns, supervisor readiness, and control effectiveness.

## Step 8: Expand or Constrain Authority

Increase authority only when evidence supports progressive delegation.

Reduce authority when performance, controls, or operating conditions deteriorate.

## Step 9: Reassess Continuously

Review the agent after material changes, incidents, scope expansion, or at defined intervals.

Digital workforce governance should mature through practical experience, not theoretical confidence alone.

# 18. Practical Questions for Leaders and Supervisors

Before deploying or expanding an AI agent, organizations should ask:

1. What work is the agent being asked to perform?
2. Who is accountable for the outcome?
3. What authority is being delegated?
4. Which systems, data, and tools can the agent access?
5. What actions can it execute without approval?
6. How will supervisors know what the agent has done?
7. What could happen if the agent is wrong?
8. Can its actions be reversed?
9. Can execution be paused or stopped immediately?
10. Are supervisors capable of recognizing abnormal behavior?
11. Has the agent's reliability been demonstrated in this operating context?
12. Has the organization's management capability matured enough for the proposed level of autonomy?
13. What event would cause the agent's authority to be reduced?
14. When will the deployment be reviewed again?

If these questions cannot be answered clearly, the organization is not yet ready to delegate the proposed authority.

# 19. Common Governance Failures

Organizations should avoid the following patterns.

## Treating Technical Access as Authority

An integration may make an action possible without making it legitimate or approved.

## Allowing Agents to Inherit Human Permissions

Human accounts often contain broader access than an agent requires and weaken traceability.

## Assigning Accountability Without Intervention Rights

A supervisor cannot manage an agent effectively without visibility and the ability to stop or override it.

## Scaling Before Learning

Expanding an agent's scope before reliability, monitoring, and supervisory capability are established creates a governance mismatch.

## Measuring Outputs but Ignoring Actions

For agentic systems, the path taken and actions executed may be as important as the final output.

## Treating Approval as Permanent

A deployment that was appropriate at launch may become inappropriate after changes in capability, access, data, or business context.

## Assuming More Autonomy Is Always Better

The objective is not maximum autonomy. It is effective and accountable performance within the organization's management capability.

# 20. Applying This Framework

Every organization will adopt agentic AI differently.

Some may begin with narrow internal assistants.

Others may deploy agents across complex operational workflows.

Regulated or safety-critical environments may require significantly stronger controls than lower-impact internal use cases.

This framework is intended to provide a common management language across these contexts.

Organizations may use it to support:

- enterprise AI strategy;
- agent deployment standards;
- governance reviews;
- operating-model design;
- risk and control assessments;
- training for managers and users;
- internal audit;
- vendor evaluation; and
- continuous improvement of AI-enabled workflows.

The framework should be adapted rather than adopted mechanically.

Its central requirement is that agent authority, human accountability, and organizational control remain aligned.

---

# Closing Statement

Organizations have spent generations learning how to assign work, delegate authority, supervise performance, manage exceptions, and hold people accountable.

Agentic AI introduces a new management challenge.

Organizations must now apply these disciplines to systems capable of performing work alongside human teams.

The future of AI governance is therefore not only about governing technology.

It is about governing work.

AI agents may plan, execute, communicate, and operate across systems, but they do not assume responsibility for organizational outcomes.

That responsibility remains with people.

> **AI agents may perform work, but only people remain accountable for the work performed.**
